Overview

PO Copilot is an agentic AI workflow I built to help myself write better Feature PRDs and User Stories, ask the questions I don't always think to ask myself, generate my analytics intake documents, and post everything straight to Azure DevOps without opening a browser. What started as a personal tool grew, one real problem at a time, into something that also checks drafts against our team's Business Rules and Definition of Ready, and it's now being adopted by Product Owners across the company through the Dojo, the company's internal product and technical training program.

"The AI drafts, I approve, then it posts — nothing reaches the backlog without a human saying so."

Origin

I care about writing good PRDs and clear user stories. That's a personal standard, not something anyone assigned me, but even holding myself to it, I'd still miss things.

One example that stuck with me: we needed to raise marketing opt-in numbers while staying compliant with GDPR for non-US users. My first instinct was to key off geolocation or IP address, but that falls apart fast. What if someone downloads the app while visiting the US, gets flagged as a US user, then flies home to Europe? Now they're under GDPR, but our system is still treating them like a US person. Add a VPN into the mix while they're in Europe, and it gets messier still. Policy and legal were going to be reviewing this, so I needed to be thorough about every edge case, not just the obvious one.

That's the kind of thing I wanted help catching. Not because I couldn't do it myself, but because I know I don't always think of every angle alone, and I wanted to get better at that.

I'd been playing with Claude Code on side projects for a while, building small agentic workflows for myself. When I got a Copilot license at work, that was the moment. I could finally build this for real work and actually share it with people, not just experiment on my own time.

The one thing I was firm about from the start: I didn't want the AI filling in gaps on its own. I've seen AI hallucinate and take liberties enough times to know that if you let it guess, it will, and it'll sound confident doing it. I learned that if you have it ask clarifying questions instead of assuming, you get a much better result. That became the whole premise of the tool: brain dump first, then let the AI interrogate me instead of the other way around.

Building It

The first version was just the core workflow: brain dump, then clarifying questions. I'd tell it my goal, my concerns, and my first-pass idea for a solution. On the GDPR example, I'd explain what I wanted, and it would come back with things like: what about people using a VPN for personal privacy, or frequent travelers whose location changes between when they open the app and when a rule needs to apply. The end result followed the standard format: a PRD for Features, and for User Stories, "As a / I want / So that" with Given/When/Then acceptance criteria.

Once that was working, I realized my analytics intake was next. It wasn't that I was bad at it, it was that it was tedious enough that I kept putting it off. So I trained the workflow on the templates our analytics team needed and had it generate the actual documents, a feature flow doc and an analytics questions and observation requirements spreadsheet, straight from the same brain dump and conversation.

That felt like a good stopping point, but then I thought about how much time I was losing to a different kind of busywork. At the company, a Feature typically breaks into three or four User Stories, and each of those needs to exist once per platform, iOS and Android, each with its own tags for that platform's dev team. So a Feature that started as three or four stories turned into six to eight once you accounted for platforms, all duplicated by hand: copy the story, retitle it, re-tag it, every time. I got Azure DevOps integrated using a PAT and had the workflow post directly and handle the duplication itself. What used to be manual copy-paste for every platform now happens instantly from a single brain dump, I just confirm the content before it posts.

Around this time, the team put together a formal Definition of Ready: the things a story needed before a dev could pick it up without having to ask questions, like acceptance criteria, what happens if it fails, design mocks done, analytics done, and a few other categories. I trained the workflow on it. It didn't change the fact that it was still asking questions, but now those questions were aimed at the DoR specifically, grading the draft against it and asking follow-ups to close any gaps, most often around acceptance criteria and failure cases.

Then I found out our Business Rules were sitting in an outdated Word document, forty pages of it, and the team needed it migrated to BookStack. I did the migration myself, organizing it into shelves, books, chapters, and pages, then trained the workflow on it so it could check whatever I was drafting against the actual rules and flag anything that conflicted. I also wanted to learn RAG properly, and this was a real problem I could apply it to, which is how I learn best. Building real retrieval also kept the workflow from being token-heavy, since it wasn't dumping the whole document into every conversation. I ended up with two passes:

Fetch → Parse → Chunk → Embed → Index → Search

Keyword search against BookStack's API is the fast first pass. Semantic search is the backup, which matters most for newer POs who don't know the exact wording a rule uses yet, since it's a much better experience than something that only works if you already know the keyword.

Last was making it shareable. Not everyone is in the same place with AI, especially CLI tools, and most people aren't familiar with setting up a PAT or an API integration. I knew that if onboarding was hard, it wouldn't matter how good the workflow was, people would drop off before they ever used it. So I built a walkthrough for non-technical setup on both Mac and Windows. The company is large enough that I don't mind personally walking people through it, but self-service had to work too, or adoption would depend entirely on my bandwidth.

How It Works Today

Two paths through the system now, depending on whether I'm framing a broad initiative or a scoped piece of work. Both start with a plain-English brain dump, both get interrupted by the same two safety nets, and both converge on the same human gate.

Starting point “I have an idea…”

Feature Flow

A big idea or initiative that will become several pieces of work

  1. Brain dump the idea — freely, no structure required
  2. Clarifying questions, one at a time — who's affected, what success looks like, what's out of scope
  3. First draft written and saved locally
  4. Sounding-board pass — the AI reads the draft back and probes for use cases and edge cases you didn't consider
  5. Business Rule checkBoth flows
  6. Analytics checkBoth flows
  7. Parent Epic link — the ID is validated before anything is attached
  8. Final review, together
  9. Optional: break it into User Stories — hands off to the User Story flow, one story at a time

User Story Flow

A specific, scoped, sprint-sized piece of work

  1. Brain dump the story — standalone, or handed over from a Feature
  2. Definition of Ready questions, one at a time — the specific user, the flow, acceptance criteria in Given/When/Then, what's out of scope. Mobile stories add a completeness pass for platforms, dark mode, accessibility, and error handling
  3. First draft written and saved locally
  4. Analytics checkBoth flows
  5. Parent Feature link — the ID is validated before anything is attached

Business Rule Validation

Fires automatically mid-draft — keyword plus semantic search catches conflicts even when the brain dump doesn't use the rule's exact wording, and the resolution gets written into the draft.

Analytics Form Automation

Also automatic, once a draft is solid. A short set of questions produces two real, ready-to-share files — nothing is submitted anywhere; the PO shares them however their process works.

Two details in that diagram carry most of the intent behind it. The Feature flow deliberately isn't a gated checklist. A Feature is a document to get right, so the AI plays sounding board instead of gatekeeper. The User Story flow is the opposite, it ends in a pass/fail grade, because a story is a commitment a team will act on. Same system, two different postures, chosen per artifact.

Design Principles

A few rules I held myself to, deliberately, from early on:

  • Make me think, don't think for me. The whole point was to ask me questions that made me consider the feature or solution more carefully, not to do the brain work for me. That's my job. The AI's job is to automate the tedious parts and help me think, not replace the thinking.
  • Never let it assume. If I hadn't said it, the AI didn't get to invent it. Gaps became questions, not guesses.
  • Drafts, not actions. This was a decision from the start: the AI drafts, I approve, then it posts. It never touches Azure DevOps on its own.
  • Use what people already have. I built this on our existing Copilot license instead of a new vendor or a separate tool. A new portal in a new space is just another thing people have to learn, and it wouldn't get adopted. This way, everyone's building AI fluency on the same tool they already have access to.
  • Keep it simple enough to prove itself. I know I have a tendency to over-engineer things, and I was conscious about not letting that happen here, because adoption depended on it. Credentials are handled through system environment variables instead of pasting keys into a terminal or chat window, both for security and to teach people the right way to do it. Beyond that, I stayed away from anything fancier than it needed to be. Something simple that works and proves its value beats something impressive that never gets used.

Rollout & Adoption

I used it myself for a while before showing it to anyone else. The first person I brought it to was a Product Owner on the mobile app team. I led with the two things I knew hit the biggest pain points for POs, business rules and analytics form generation, and once she saw those, she wanted to see the full workflow. She loved it and pushed for it to roll out to the rest of the Product Owners, figuring most people would get at least the business rules and analytics pieces out of it even if they didn't use the whole thing end to end.

From there it went to the company's Dojo, the internal program where teams come in to build product mindset and technical skills. It's mostly been developer-facing AI workflow tooling so far, nothing built for Product Owners specifically, so this was new territory for them. I sent our CTIO an email with a link to the repo, which has a full writeup of what the workflow does and how it works. He was impressed enough to share it with several IT leaders, and since then the Dojo team has been asking for demos and access so they can learn it themselves and start rolling it out to other teams.

Right now it's live with the mobile app Product Owners, about ten users, opt-in rather than mandatory. Company-wide rollout through the Dojo starts next month.

Impact & Reception

There was some initial resistance, mostly the usual reaction to "another tool" and a little intimidation about using AI this directly. That faded once people actually saw what it produced: Features and Stories posted straight to ADO without opening a browser, Business Rules referenced and updated the same way, and analytics forms generated without anyone touching a Word doc or spreadsheet by hand. The analytics piece got the loudest reaction, more than one person's response to no longer having to build those forms manually was a straight "thank you."

Devs have noticed the difference too. Stories are more complete going into refinement, and there's less of them filling in gaps or taking their own liberties with a story that was underspecified to begin with.

Analytics intake is the one place I have a real number: roughly 30–45 minutes of manual form-building down to about 5. Business Rules referencing and updating doesn't have a clean time estimate attached to it, but making that process this much easier has made it something people actually do, instead of something that gets skipped.

What This Sharpened

Technically, this was the most hands-on I've been: building a functional RAG pipeline, wiring up multiple integrations, and structuring sub-agents into an actual agentic workflow instead of a single prompt. I got to see all of it produce something my team actually uses.

It also changed how I think about the tedious parts of my own job. Busywork is what stops me from thinking creatively in the first place, until it becomes the problem I get to solve instead of the thing slowing me down.

If I'd known from the start everything this would eventually become, I probably would have written that first prompt differently. But that's what building a product actually looks like: start small, and let each new problem you run into tell you what to build next.

The clearest lesson was about building for other people versus building for myself. If I'd only ever built this for me, I wouldn't have bothered making it friendly to a wider range of people, and I definitely wouldn't have cared about a simple onboarding process the AI walks a new user through. Knowing it was going to be shared changed the decisions I made along the way, not just the finished product.

The thing I want someone reading this to take away: I notice problems and build solutions people actually adopt, because I design with the user in mind, not just the outcome I want.
