Overview

I designed and built PO Copilot — an AI workflow system that changes how Product Owners at American Airlines go from an idea to a properly formed Feature or User Story in Azure DevOps. What used to be a bare title with none of the context anyone outside the project could use, hunting for Business Rules buried in SharePoint, and analytics paperwork assembled after the fact is now a structured, AI-guided conversation that produces all three up front.

It runs entirely inside enterprise-approved AI tooling — no new vendor, no new subscription, no new credentials — and ships through a private repo any PO can set up with a single bootstrap command. The AI's operating rules are a committed file, not a vendor-portal setting: templates, the Definition of Ready as machine-readable criteria, and every rule the AI must never break live as version-controlled markdown, so every PO gets an identical AI and every behavior change is reviewed like code.

"The AI drafts, the Definition of Ready gates, and the Product Owner posts — nothing reaches the backlog without a human saying so."

The Problem

Challenge

Most Features in the backlog had a title and nothing else — no problem statement, no proposed solution, no notes on what to consider. Only the people already on the project understood what a feature actually did or why it mattered. This wasn't a lack of care: Product Owners at American Airlines carry a heavy load of responsibilities, and writing a full PRD for every feature is the first thing to get squeezed out.

That same time pressure showed up in three places at once:

  • User Stories were just as thin. Usually a name, rarely a description, and acceptance criteria even less often than on Features — which meant devs and POs re-litigated scope in every refinement.
  • Business Rules went stale and stayed hidden. POs didn't have time to keep them current, and they lived as Word documents in SharePoint — hard to find, hard to navigate, not searchable, and overwhelming to read through even when you found the right one.
  • Analytics intake happened at the wrong time. The intake process is long, and in practice its artifacts weren't built until after developers had already started — sometimes finished — the work. Analytics review would come back after dev was done, and launch slipped waiting on it.

PO Copilot is a conversation, not a form: a PO brain-dumps a rough idea in plain English and gets back a polished draft, with posting to Azure DevOps handled on command. Three workflows do the real work.

Workflow 1 — Brain Dump to a Gated Draft

Templates · Definition of Ready · Conversational Flow

Turning a bare title into a real PRD, without costing the PO an hour

The AI writes the Problem Statement, Proposed Solution, and considerations a Feature was missing — and the persona, scope, and Given/When/Then acceptance criteria a User Story was missing — by asking, not assuming. The Definition of Ready itself is encoded as machine-readable criteria, each tagged Critical or Non-critical, across product, design, technical, and team readiness, with an extra tier for mobile-facing stories.

  • Critical criteria are a hard gate. Persona, acceptance criteria, scope, and user outcome can't be skipped or left blank — the DoR is no longer a document that gets glanced at.
  • One question at a time, in plain language, instead of a blank form or a twenty-question checklist.
  • No fabrication, ever. If the PO hasn't said it, the AI doesn't write it — gaps become questions, not invented content.

Quality moves upstream: refinement stops being where a story's holes get discovered and becomes where a complete story gets discussed.

Workflow 2 — Two-Pass Business Rules Retrieval (RAG)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation · Shift-Left Compliance

Find the rule even when you don't know what it's called

Business Rules used to live as Word documents in SharePoint — hard to find, hard to navigate, not searchable, and overwhelming to work through even when you had the right one open. POs didn't have time to keep them updated, so they drifted out of date. I migrated the whole set into BookStack, a self-hosted wiki with a real API, then built retrieval and authoring on top of it so keeping rules current stops being a task nobody has time for.

While a PO drafts, the AI searches Business Rules for anything relevant to what's being built and surfaces conflicts before they reach development. The first pass is a keyword search against BookStack's API: fast, exact, zero cost. But keyword search only works if you already know the official terminology — exactly the knowledge a newer PO doesn't have. So I built a second, semantic pass — a full retrieval-augmented generation pipeline over the Business Rules corpus:

Fetch → Parse → Chunk → Embed → Index → Search
  • Fetch & parse pulls every page from BookStack via API and converts it to clean plain text.
  • Chunk splits pages into overlapping, paragraph-aware windows so a rule spanning a section break isn't cut in half.
  • Embed calls an embeddings model through the same enterprise-approved AI trust boundary the team already uses — no new vendor, no new credential.
  • Index stores chunks and vectors in a local, gitignored SQLite file; an incremental refresh only re-embeds pages that actually changed.
  • Search ranks by cosine similarity and returns the top pages by best-matching chunk.

A PO can search "letting someone move seats after boarding" and get back the Seat Change Request rules, even though none of those words are in the page title. When a rule conflicts with what's being drafted, the AI stops, asks the PO which one should change, and documents the resolution with a citation back to the source page.

Retrieval runs in the other direction too. The AI follows the team's own standard for how a Business Rule should be written, so when a PO creates or updates a rule — right from inside the drafting conversation, instead of as a separate task — the result already matches house format instead of needing a rewrite from whoever owns the rule.

Workflow 3 — Analytics Intake Generation

Document Automation · Institutional Memory

Fixing the timing, not just the typing

Analytics intake was arriving at the wrong point in the process — built after developers had already started, sometimes finished, the work, so analytics review landed after dev was done and pushed launch out. Moving intake into the drafting conversation fixes the sequencing: it's ready to submit the moment the Feature or Story exists, before a developer picks it up.

Once a draft is final, the AI asks whether it needs analytics tracking, walks a structured intake — scope, timeline, flow steps, the stakeholder questions and KPIs the analytics team needs — and generates two actual files, not a block of text to copy in: a flow document and a stakeholder workbook, in the exact format the analytics team expects.

  • Contacts are asked once. Product Owner, Product Analyst, Program Manager, and lead developer are captured on first run and auto-fill every form after that.
  • Real documents, real formats. A Python generator produces the Word and Excel deliverables from structured data, shareable the moment they exist.

The visible number is roughly 30–45 minutes of manual form-filling down to about five. The bigger fix is that those five minutes now happen before dev starts, not after — which is what actually protects the launch date.

Two paths through the system, depending on whether a PO is framing a broad initiative or a scoped piece of work. Both start with a plain-English brain dump, both get interrupted by the same two safety nets, and both converge on the same human gate.

Fires automatically mid-draft — keyword plus semantic search catches conflicts even when the brain dump doesn't use the rule's exact wording, and the resolution gets written into the draft.

Two details in that diagram carry most of the design intent. The Feature flow deliberately isn't a gated checklist — a Feature is a document to get right, so the AI plays sounding board instead of gatekeeper. The User Story flow is the opposite: it ends in a pass/fail grade because a story is a commitment a team will act on. Same system, two different postures, chosen per artifact.

Architecture & Design Decisions

The decisions worth defending here were about trust and adoption more than about models:

  • The system prompt is a committed file, not vendor-portal configuration — reproducible, diffable, and reviewed through pull requests like any other code. Every PO gets the same AI.
  • Draft first, post on command. The AI never writes to Azure DevOps unless told to; drafts land locally and gitignored. That single rule is why POs trust it with real work.
  • Semantic search augments, never replaces. Keyword search stays the fast first pass; semantic retrieval merges in as a second pass. A PO who skips the index setup loses nothing but the paraphrase-matching upgrade — adoption stays optional, not all-or-nothing.
  • Reuse the trust boundary you already have. The retrieval layer runs on the enterprise-approved AI tooling's existing auth — no new vendor review, no new credentials. The elegant architecture that needs six months of security approval ships later than the good one that doesn't.
  • Boring storage on purpose. A local SQLite file with brute-force cosine similarity is more than fast enough at this corpus size and trivially gitignore-safe. A vector database would have been more impressive and strictly worse.
  • Adoption was a design constraint, not an afterthought. A seven-step manual setup became one bootstrap command, with a written onboarding path for both Mac and Windows and graceful degradation — if the embeddings service isn't reachable, keyword search still works. Every credential is a shell environment variable, verified by length only, never logged or committed.

Impact

The system is live and shared across the team. Analytics intake is the clearest single number — roughly 30–45 minutes collapsed to about five, and moved ahead of dev instead of after it, so it no longer delays launch. The harder-to-measure change: Business Rules are current and searchable for the first time, and conflicts that used to surface in QA or post-launch now surface while the story is still being written.

What This Project Sharpened

  • Fixing timing, not just typing: the analytics intake problem wasn't effort, it was sequencing — recognizing that and moving the work earlier mattered more than automating the form itself.
  • Turning process into product: re-expressing a Definition of Ready that lived as a document into executable criteria with enforceable gates. Process only changes behavior when it's in the path of the work.
  • Shipping inside enterprise constraints: approved tooling only, no new vendors, no new credentials — treating those as design inputs, not obstacles, while still designing for trust: no fabrication, cited sources, drafts before actions.
