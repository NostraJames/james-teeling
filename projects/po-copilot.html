Overview

I designed and built PO Copilot — an AI workflow system that changes how Product Owners at a major US airline go from an idea to a properly formed Feature or User Story in Azure DevOps. What used to be hours of staring at a blank work-item form, hunting for Business Rules in an internal wiki, re-typing the same stakeholder contacts into analytics intake forms, and trying to remember every Definition of Ready criterion is now a structured, AI-guided conversation.

The system runs entirely inside the enterprise-approved AI tooling — no new vendor, no new subscription, no new credentials. It's shared with the team through a private repo, and a non-technical PO can go from clone to fully set up with a single bootstrap command. The interesting engineering isn't the prompting; it's the retrieval layer, the gates, and the fact that the whole AI's behavior is a version-controlled file.

"The AI drafts, the Definition of Ready gates, and the Product Owner posts — nothing reaches the backlog without a human saying so."

The Problem

Challenge

Every Product Owner faces the same friction going from an idea to a backlog-ready work item. The form is blank and offers no guidance. The Definition of Ready lives in a document nobody re-reads, so stories arrive at refinement half-formed. The company's Business Rules are spread across a wiki of ten-plus books, so rule conflicts surface in QA — or in production — instead of during drafting. And every analytics request means filling the same intake forms from scratch, by hand.

The specific failure points I set out to solve:

  • Unenforced Definition of Ready: stories entered sprint planning missing a persona, acceptance criteria, or a defined scope — and the delay showed up downstream, not at authoring time.
  • Undiscoverable Business Rules: a PO could only find a rule if they already knew its official terminology. Conflicts got caught late, when they were expensive.
  • Repeated manual grind: analytics intake was roughly 30–45 minutes of form-filling per item, redone from scratch and inconsistently each time.
  • No memory between sessions: the same clarifying questions got asked — or missed — over and over.
  • Technical setup as an adoption barrier: a productivity tool that needs a developer to install is a tool most Product Owners will never adopt.

The Solution: An Opinionated AI Teammate

PO Copilot is a conversation, not a form. A PO opens their terminal, brain-dumps a rough idea in plain English, and gets back a polished draft — persona and acceptance criteria enforced, relevant Business Rules cross-checked and cited, analytics intake documents generated, and posting to Azure DevOps handled on command.

What makes it a system rather than a prompt is that the AI's operating rules are a committed file in the repo, not settings in a vendor portal. Templates, the Definition of Ready encoded as machine-readable criteria, the conversational flows, and the Golden Rules the AI must never break all live as version-controlled markdown. Any PO who pulls the repo gets an identical AI, and every change to its behavior is diffable and reviewed like code.

On top of that foundation, three workflows do the real work.

Workflow 1 — Brain Dump to a Gated Draft

Templates · Definition of Ready · Conversational Flow

The Definition of Ready as executable criteria, not a document

The foundation is a set of structured templates — a PRD-shaped Feature (Problem Statement, Proposed Solution, Expected Results) and a User Story in Connextra format with acceptance criteria always written Given / When / Then. But the templates aren't the interesting part. The interesting part is that the Definition of Ready is encoded as machine-readable criteria, each tagged Critical or Non-critical, spanning product and business readiness, design, technical, requirements, and team tiers — plus a conditional tier that only activates for mobile-facing stories, covering platforms, dark mode, accessibility, error handling, and test plan.

  • Critical criteria are a hard gate. Persona, acceptance criteria, scope, and user outcome cannot be skipped or left blank. Before this, the DoR was a document that got glanced at; now it's something the draft has to pass through.
  • One question at a time. The conversational flow enforces a single clarifying question per turn, with predefined phrasing, ordering, and an edge-case taxonomy. This is the difference between handing someone a twenty-question form and talking it through with them.
  • No fabrication, ever. If the PO hasn't said it, the AI doesn't write it. Gaps become questions, not invented content.

The result is that quality moves upstream. Instead of refinement being where a story's holes get discovered, it's where a complete story gets discussed.

Workflow 2 — Two-Pass Business Rules Retrieval (RAG)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation · Shift-Left Compliance

Find the rule even when you don't know what it's called

While a PO drafts, the AI searches the company's Business Rules for anything relevant to what's being built — and surfaces conflicts before they reach development. The first pass is a keyword search against the wiki's REST API: fast, exact, zero cost. But keyword search only works if you already know the official terminology, which is exactly the knowledge a newer PO doesn't have.

So I built a second, semantic pass — a full retrieval-augmented generation pipeline over the Business Rules corpus:

Fetch → Parse → Chunk → Embed → Index → Search
  • Fetch & parse pulls every page from the Business Rules shelf via API and converts HTML to clean plain text.
  • Chunk splits pages into overlapping, paragraph-boundary-aware windows so a rule that spans a section break isn't cut in half.
  • Embed calls an embeddings model through the same enterprise-approved AI trust boundary the team already uses — deliberately reusing the existing auth token rather than introducing a new vendor or a new credential to get approved.
  • Index stores chunks and vectors as blobs in a local SQLite file that is gitignored and never committed — internal content stays on the PO's machine. An incremental refresh compares update timestamps and only re-embeds pages that actually changed.
  • Search ranks by cosine similarity and returns the top pages by best-matching chunk.

The practical effect: a PO can search "letting someone move seats after boarding" and get back the Seat Change Request rules — even though none of those words appear in the page title. When a rule conflicts with what's being drafted, the AI stops, asks the PO to resolve the contradiction, and documents the resolution in the draft with a citation back to the source page.

Retrieval also runs in the other direction. When a Business Rule needs to be created or updated as part of a review, the AI first consults the company's own rule-authoring standard, so any new rule content it drafts matches the house format before a human rule owner ever sees it.

Workflow 3 — Analytics Intake Generation

Document Automation · Institutional Memory

From a finished draft to real, ready-to-send files

Once a Feature or Story draft is final, the AI asks whether it needs analytics tracking. If it does, it walks a structured intake — summary, scope, timeline, flow steps, and the stakeholder questions, decisions, and KPIs the analytics team needs — and then generates two actual files, not a copy-pasteable block of text: a flow document and a stakeholder workbook, both matching the exact format the analytics team expects.

  • Contacts are asked once. Product Owner, Product Analyst, Program Manager, and lead developer are captured on first run and saved to a local config, so they auto-fill on every future form. That's the institutional memory that was missing before.
  • Real documents, real formats. A Python generator produces the Word and Excel deliverables from structured data, so the output is shareable the moment it exists.

This collapsed roughly 30–45 minutes of manual, error-prone form-filling per item into about five minutes that happens inside the drafting conversation instead of after it.

How It Fits Together

There isn't one path through the system — there are two, and which one a PO lands on depends on whether they're framing a broad initiative or a scoped piece of work. Both open the same way, with a plain-English brain dump. Both get interrupted by the same two safety nets. And both converge on the same single human gate.

Business Rule Validation

Never run on its own — it fires automatically mid-draft. Exact keyword search plus a local semantic pass catches rules described in different words than the brain dump used. If a rule conflicts with what's being proposed, the AI stops and asks which one should change: the rule, or the feature. The answer is written into the draft.

Analytics Form Automation

Also automatic, once a draft is solid. A short set of questions — what's tracked, which platforms, the step-by-step user flow, the stakeholder questions the data should answer — produces two real, ready-to-share files. Nothing is submitted anywhere; the PO shares them however their process works.

Two details in that diagram carry most of the design intent. The Feature flow deliberately isn't a gated checklist — a Feature is a document to get right, so the AI plays sounding board instead of gatekeeper. The User Story flow is the opposite: it ends in a pass/fail grade because a story is a commitment a team will act on. Same system, two different postures, chosen per artifact.

Built for Adoption, Not Just for Me

A tool only one person can install isn't a system — it's a personal script. Most of the hardest work in this build went into making it something a non-technical Product Owner could adopt without borrowing a developer.

  • A written onboarding path covering access-token creation and API credential setup on both Mac and Windows, step by step, with the same safe-credential pattern each time.
  • One-command bootstrap for the semantic search setup. What started as a seven-step manual process became a single script that installs Python dependencies, installs the CLI (trying each of the common Windows package managers in turn, and Homebrew on Mac), triggers sign-in if needed, tests access, and builds the first index.
  • Graceful degradation. If the embeddings service isn't available, the bootstrap exits cleanly and the PO still has full keyword search. Nobody is left with a broken tool.
  • Secret hygiene by design. Every credential is a shell environment variable. Nothing is printed, logged, or committed. Setup is verified by checking a token's length, never its value, and the onboarding explicitly tells POs to type tokens into their terminal rather than into a chat window.

Architecture & Design Decisions

The decisions worth defending in this build were about trust and adoption more than about models:

  • The system prompt is a committed file. Encoding the AI's operating rules as version-controlled markdown instead of vendor-portal configuration means behavior is reproducible, auditable, diffable, and reviewed through pull requests like any other code. Every PO gets the same AI.
  • Draft first, post on command. The AI never writes to Azure DevOps unless explicitly told to. Drafts land locally and gitignored. That single rule removes the anxiety of AI-generated content ending up somewhere unintended — and it's why POs trust it with real work.
  • Semantic search is additive, never a replacement. Keyword search stays the first pass because it's fast, exact, and free; semantic retrieval is a second pass merged and de-duplicated by page URL. A PO who never sets up the index loses nothing — they just don't get the paraphrase-matching upgrade. Choosing "augment" over "replace" kept the fast path fast and made adoption optional rather than all-or-nothing.
  • Reuse the trust boundary you already have. Building the retrieval layer on the enterprise-approved AI tooling's existing auth meant no new vendor review, no new credentials, and no new attack surface. The most elegant architecture that needs a six-month security approval ships later than the good one that doesn't.
  • Boring storage on purpose. A local SQLite file with brute-force cosine similarity is more than fast enough at this corpus size, needs zero services, and is trivially gitignore-safe. A vector database would have been more impressive and strictly worse.

Impact

The system is live and shared across the team. The clearest single number is analytics intake — roughly 30–45 minutes of manual form-filling collapsed to about five, and moved inside the drafting flow rather than after it. The larger change is harder to put a number on: Business Rule conflicts that used to surface in QA or post-launch now surface while the story is still being written.

What This Project Sharpened

  • Retrieval engineering: building a working RAG pipeline end to end — chunking strategy, embedding, incremental re-indexing, and similarity ranking — and knowing when brute force is the right answer.
  • Turning process into product: taking a Definition of Ready that existed as a document and re-expressing it as executable criteria with enforceable gates. Process only changes behavior when it's in the path of the work.
  • Designing for trust: no fabrication, cited sources, drafts before actions, and a human key on anything that writes to a system of record.
  • Shipping inside enterprise constraints: approved tooling only, no new vendors, no new credentials — and treating those constraints as design inputs rather than obstacles.
  • Developer experience for non-developers: collapsing a seven-step setup into one command, across two operating systems and corporate IT restrictions, because adoption is a feature.
  • Dogfooding my own role: I built this on my own team, for the Product Owners I work alongside, on the exact pain I felt every sprint — which is the shortest possible feedback loop between problem and solution.
